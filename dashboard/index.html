Chart guide · five-vintage target-month journeys

How to read the balanced revision effectiveness score

This chart asks whether each new forecast version made the forecast meaningfully better—not merely whether it changed.

Two related measures, two different questions Revision effectiveness % counts how many material revisions helped. This balanced score measures how much error was removed, how efficiently, and how consistently.
01

Read one month as a journey

Every target month starts again at a neutral 50.

Step up: the new vintage helped Step down: it added error Outlined point: latest vintage
02

Three ingredients build the score

Each ingredient is centred on 50 and bounded 0–100.

50%Accuracy gain

How much of V1’s starting absolute error was removed?

V1 errorerror removed
30%Revision efficiency

How much error was removed per 1 KL of total movement?

forecast movement→error removed
20%Consistency

Were movement-weighted vintage steps mostly helpful?

Overall=50% accuracy+30% efficiency+20% consistency→0–100
03

Interpret level and shape together

The destination matters, but so does the path.

80–100Highly effectiveLarge, efficient and dependable improvement
60–79EffectiveRevisions produced useful net improvement
40–59MixedInspect whipsaw, timing and weak signals
0–39HarmfulRevisions added material forecast error
↗ Steady rise Preserve the signals and process. ↕ Whipsaw Test cadence, assumptions and ownership. ↘ Decline Find which vintage introduced error.
04

Turn the pattern into a better review

Use the chart to examine decision quality and timing—not to grade an individual planner from one score.

With the forecasting team
  • Which vintage created the largest rise or fall?
  • What signal, override or assumption caused it?
  • Could the useful information have arrived earlier?
With the business team
  • What business assumption changed between vintages?
  • Was the amount moved proportionate to the evidence?
  • What decision or input should change next cycle?

Chart guide · parent-level revision behaviour

How to read parent vintage trend vs improvement score

Each bubble connects the direction forecasts moved with whether that movement made the forecast more accurate.

One bubble is one parent code Six complete target months×Five vintages per month→Median parent behaviour
01

Start with the two axes

Direction is horizontal; decision quality is vertical.

Above zero revisions improved accuracy Below zero revisions worsened accuracy Darker heat cell more parent bubbles occupy that area
02

How one bubble is calculated

The chart looks for a repeatable trend, not one isolated edit.

1For each target month, line up V1 → V5.
2Measure the forecast-level trend as % of actual per vintage.
3Measure the accuracy trend as forecast-accuracy points per vintage.
4Take the median across six months to place the parent bubble.
X · Forecast trend

Positive: forecasts rose through the vintages.

Negative: forecasts fell through the vintages.

Y · Improvement score

Positive: forecast accuracy improved.

Negative: forecast accuracy deteriorated.

X is not bias and Y is not the balanced 0–100 score. Both are slopes across vintages, summarized by the six-month median.

03

Read bubbles and heatmap together

Separate individual parents from the population’s common pattern.

Good patternUpper half, repeated across months. Preserve the signal and timing.
Watch closelyNear zero or dense clusters. The process may be stable but not learning much.
Bad patternLower half—especially large-error or extreme bubbles. Revisions repeatedly added error.
Heatmap = concentration, not performance

The plot is divided into 14 × 8 cells. Every parent bubble adds one count to its cell; darker teal means more parents there relative to the busiest cell.

Use the controls intentionally Density turns the heatmap layer on or off; it counts parents, not KL or impact. Size by error is the default; it shows the latest vintage’s absolute error summed across six target months. Size by volume / Uniform dots switches to commercial scale or removes size encoding. Top volume / Outliers hides bubbles while retaining the heatmap as population context. SKU class changes the plotted population and its heatmap.

A dark cell is not automatically good or bad—its vertical position provides that meaning. The heatmap can also hide overlapping parents, so use bubbles and selected evidence before agreeing an action.

04

Use the quadrant to structure the conversation

Start with what moved, then test why it helped or hurt.

With the forecasting team
  • Which parents show a repeatable helpful or harmful trend?
  • Did the useful signal arrive early enough to act on?
  • Do outliers reflect process, data quality or a one-off event?
With the business team
  • What assumption explains the direction of movement?
  • Which high-error parents deserve decision priority?
  • What input or commitment should change next cycle?

Metric guide · active comparison population

How to read this metric

The question it answers Did Vintage B reduce forecast error relative to Vintage A, after scaling the result by actual demand?
01

Read the number as error removed

Positive percentage points mean the later vintage was more accurate.

Vintage A errorstarting error
Vintage B errorsmaller
the gap is error removed
Example · +4.6 ppVintage B removed absolute error equal to 4.6% of the population’s actual volume.
02

How it is calculated

All complete product-target-month pairs contribute.

Σ |A − actual|−Σ |B − actual|÷Σ actual× 100
1. Find absolute error for A and B in each pair. 2. Add the errors across the active population. 3. Divide net error removed by total actual volume.

This is population-weighted: large-volume observations influence the result more. It is not an average of SKU accuracy percentages.

03

What good and bad look like

Direction is clear; context explains whether the gain is repeatable.

PositiveB removed net error
Near zeroLittle net change or gains offset losses
NegativeB added net error

Oldest and latest available forecasts can represent different horizons by product-month unless exact horizons are selected. A positive total can also hide pockets that worsened.

04

Use it to discuss the value of revision

Pair the aggregate result with the action queue before deciding what to repeat.

With the forecasting team
  • Which products created most of the net gain or loss?
  • Was improvement consistent across horizons?
  • Which late signal could have been used earlier?
With the business team
  • Which changed assumption produced measurable accuracy value?
  • Did better accuracy arrive in time for a decision?
  • Where did offsetting losses occur?
The question it answers When the forecast was meaningfully changed, how often did that change move closer to actual demand?
01

Read it as a hit rate

Every materially revised pair gets one vote, regardless of volume.

Example · 58.3%About 58 of every 100 meaningful revisions improved accuracy; roughly 42 did not.
02

How it is calculated

Unchanged or immaterial pairs stay out of the denominator.

Revised pairs that improved÷All materially revised pairs× 100
Improved: B’s absolute error is meaningfully lower than A’s. Worsened: B’s absolute error is meaningfully higher. Neutral: the forecast moved, but accuracy barely changed.

This is count-based, not volume-weighted. A tiny product and a major product each count as one pair.

03

What good and bad look like

Higher is better, but magnitude belongs in the other two KPIs.

Clearly above 50%Most meaningful revisions helped
Around 50%Mixed process; inspect where wins and losses occur
Below 50%More meaningful revisions failed than helped

A high hit rate can still deliver little value if gains are tiny. A lower hit rate can coexist with positive net improvement if a few large gains dominate. Always triangulate all three cards.

04

Use it to discuss process reliability

Ask why revision decisions succeed or fail repeatedly.

With the forecasting team
  • Which revision types have the strongest hit rate?
  • Are failures concentrated by planner, horizon or product?
  • Which checks would prevent avoidable harmful moves?
With the business team
  • Which inputs consistently improve the forecast?
  • Where are assumption changes creating noise?
  • What evidence should be required before a late override?
The question it answers How many KL of absolute forecast error did Vintage B remove—or add—across the complete comparison population?
01

Read it as a net error ledger

Helpful and harmful pairs are added together in physical volume.

+820 +590 −430 +730 −290 individual pair contributions → net total
Example · +1,943.1 KLAfter gains and losses were combined, Vintage B removed 1,943.1 KL of absolute error.
02

How it is calculated

Each complete pair contributes a positive or negative KL amount.

For every pairΣ(|A − actual| − |B − actual|)
Positive contribution: B has less absolute error. Negative contribution: B has more absolute error. Net total: all helpful and harmful contributions combined.

The unit is KL, so the number grows with both impact and population size. Use accuracy delta when you need a normalized percentage-point comparison.

03

What good and bad look like

Magnitude shows business impact, not how consistently the process worked.

PositiveNet absolute error was removed
Near zeroLimited impact or gains offset losses
NegativeNet absolute error was added

One large-volume gain can mask many smaller failures, and one major loss can overwhelm many small wins. Pair this with revision effectiveness to separate magnitude from frequency.

04

Use it to discuss commercial impact

Trace the net result back to the parents and decisions that created it.

With the forecasting team
  • Which parents contributed most to error removed or added?
  • Were the largest losses detectable before the revision?
  • Which successful patterns should be repeated?
With the business team
  • What is the operational value of the KL error removed?
  • Which high-volume assumption changes drove the result?
  • Where should accountability or inputs change?

Guide · accuracy chart common cohort

How to read this metric

The question it answers Across the accuracy chart’s common cohort, how large was the KPI vintage’s absolute error relative to actual demand?
01

Read it as 100% minus the error burden

Higher means the chart’s KPI vintage was closer to actual demand.

Large parenterr 18 / 90 KL
Small parenterr 5 / 10 KL
Populationerr 23 / 100 KL
bars show each row’s error share of its actual volume
Example · 77%The population carried 23 KL of absolute error for every 100 KL of actual demand. It does not mean 77% of forecasts were correct.
02

Add first, divide once

One population ratio, not an average of products.

100−Σ |forecast − actual|÷Σ actual× 100
1. Keep complete pairs with a positive actual. 2. Take each pair’s absolute error in KL. 3. Sum errors and actuals, then form one ratio.

This is 100% − WAPE. It is not MAPE and not an average of SKU accuracies. Missing and zero actuals do not enter the ratio.

03

What good and bad look like

Judge thresholds against your own target, not a universal rule.

100%Perfect: no absolute error
Closer to 100%Less error per unit of actual
0%Error equals total actual volume
Below 0%Valid: error exceeded actual volume

No universal “good” cutoff exists. Check coverage before celebrating a high score, and use Bias for direction—the accuracy score cannot say over or under.

With the business team
